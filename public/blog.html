ATS·7 min read

Why your resume gets rejected in under a second

Modern hiring platforms don't wait for a human to glance at your file. Here's the actual sequence a resume goes through before anyone decides you're worth an interview.

Most job seekers picture an "ATS" as a simple keyword filter — a robot skimming for buzzwords. That's outdated. In 2026, a resume typically passes through three or four automated layers before a person ever opens it: a parser that extracts your text and structure, a keyword/skills matcher scored against the job requisition, and increasingly an LLM-based screener that reads the whole document and writes a plain-language summary and fit score for the recruiter.

The parser matters most, and it's the easiest to get wrong. Multi-column layouts, text boxes, tables and graphics can scramble the order your experience is read in, or drop it entirely. A resume that looks polished in a PDF viewer can still land on a recruiter's desk as garbled text.

Three things consistently separate resumes that clear this gate from ones that don't:

  • Single-column, standard section headings (Experience, Education, Skills) that any parser recognizes
  • Language that mirrors the target job posting, not just your internal job title
  • Complete, plain-text contact information — no contact details locked inside a header/footer or image

Hiring market·5 min read

More job postings, fewer hires — what changed

The U.S. hire rate fell to 3.1% in early 2026, a five-year low, even with millions of open roles. It's not that companies stopped hiring — it's that the funnel got longer.

Two forces are compounding. First, generative AI made applying nearly free — candidates now submit to dozens of roles in the time it used to take to tailor one resume, which means popular listings can draw 50% more applications than in prior years. Volume went up; genuine fit did not.

Second, employers responded by adding more automated filtering rather than more recruiters. The result is a narrower, more mechanical first gate for everyone — including strong candidates who simply didn't format or word their resume the way the new systems expect.

The practical takeaway: in a tighter market, a resume that's precisely tailored to one role will consistently outperform ten generic ones sent to ten roles.

LLM screening·6 min read

Keyword stuffing doesn't work anymore. Here's why.

Workday, Greenhouse and Ashby have all shipped LLM-based screening that reads a resume more like a person than a keyword scanner — which changes what "optimizing" a resume actually means.

The old advice — repeat the job title fifteen times, paste the skills list verbatim — was built for a purely lexical matcher. LLM screeners instead summarize your experience, judge whether your accomplishments plausibly match the role's scope, and draft recruiter-facing notes. Stuffed, repetitive text reads as noise to a model the same way it reads as noise to a human, and can actively hurt your summary.

What still works, and works better under LLM review, is specificity: real numbers, real scope, real outcomes. "Managed a team" tells a screener nothing. "Led a 6-person team to cut onboarding time 34% in two quarters" gives both a keyword match and a judgment signal a model can act on.

Strategy·4 min read

The 70-point rule of thumb, explained

Our scanner returns a 0–100 ATS score. Here's what those numbers actually mean in practice, and why the line at 70 isn't arbitrary.

Across the resumes we've scanned, a score below 70 correlates strongly with resumes that have at least one structural problem — thin keyword coverage, unquantified bullets, or a parsing risk — the kind of gap that gets a resume auto-filtered before a recruiter ever sees it. Above 70, a resume is generally clearing the automated gate; above 85, it reads as a strong, well-matched candidate to both keyword and LLM-based systems.

The score isn't a grade on your career — it's a read on how legible your career is to a machine right now. That's also why it's fixable: the underlying issues are almost always specific and addressable, not a reflection of weak experience.
